How attackers manipulate you

Phishing works because it plays on emotions and trust.

⏱️

Urgency

“Act now or your account will be closed.” This pushes people to react before they stop and think.

👔

Authority

Attackers pose as bank staff, IT support, or senior leaders to make instructions feel legitimate.

😨

Fear

Threats of suspension, legal action, or losses trigger stress and reduce careful thinking.

🎁

Reward

Promises like bonuses, prizes, or refunds lure people into quick clicks and false trust.

🤝

Familiarity

Attackers copy logos, language, and tone from brands people already know and trust.

📱

Smishing & vishing

SMS and phone calls can be just as convincing as email, especially when they use a false sense of urgency.

Recognising fake websites

Look beyond the logo. Trustworthy websites use legitimate domains and careful security cues.

bankofamerica.com

Legitimate

  • Domain matches the real organisation exactly
  • HTTPS and valid security certificate
  • Consistent branding and page layout
  • Links route to the official site
bankofamerica-update.com

Look-alike scam

  • Typo-squatting or extra words in the domain
  • Urgent warning messages or fake verification forms
  • Suspicious login page asking for credentials
  • Padlock alone does not prove legitimacy
Key rule: If a website asks for a password, code, or payment details unexpectedly, verify it by visiting the official site yourself instead of using the link in the message.

Real-world examples

Attackers often reuse the same tricks across email, social media, and phone calls.

Twitter 2020 vishing attack

Attackers impersonated employees and used social engineering to trick staff into handing over account access.

Google and Facebook invoice fraud

Scammers impersonated vendors and created fake invoices that looked legitimate, leading to large wire transfers.

2016 US campaign email

A phishing email used a familiar style and urgent language to lure people into clicking malicious links.

Best practices

Security is mostly about slowing down, verifying, and using strong defaults.

  • Pause before you click: stop, inspect the sender, and check the domain.
  • Use MFA and a password manager so stolen credentials are less valuable.
  • Verify unusual requests by contacting the organisation through its official website or phone number.
  • Report suspicious emails or texts to your security team or service provider.
  • If you clicked a suspicious link, disconnect from the network, run a malware scan, and change your passwords.
